Chapter 1

Smaller than small

How tiny atoms are, how empty they are, and the three particles they are built from. Begin by zooming in.

Pick up a single grain of table salt. It is barely a third of a millimetre wide, yet it contains about a billion billion atoms, 1018 of them, stacked in a perfect cubic lattice of sodium and chlorine. Atoms are so small that ordinary intuition gives up long before you reach them. The only way through is to go step by step.

1.1How small?

The figure below is a zoom with no cheating: every object is drawn at its true size relative to the frame. Drag the slider, scroll over the figure, or press Dive and watch the field of view shrink by a factor of ten every few seconds.

A typical atom is about a tenth of a nanometre across: 0.1 nm, or 10−10 metres. Lined up, about 750,000 hydrogen atoms would span the width of a human hair. No light microscope could ever show one, even in principle: the wavelength of visible light, 400 to 700 nm, is thousands of times larger than an atom, and a wave cannot pick out details much smaller than itself. Seeing atoms took entirely different tools, which we meet in Chapter 2.

Notice how long the zoom spends in empty haze once it passes inside the hydrogen atom. Four whole powers of ten go by with almost nothing to see before the nucleus appears. That emptiness is not an artefact of the drawing. It is the most surprising fact about atoms.

Counting them

Twelve grams of carbon, a lump about the size of a sugar cube, contains about 6.02 × 1023 atoms. Chemists call this quantity a mole, and the number is Avogadro’s number. Counting those atoms at one per second would take 19 million billion years: more than a million times the present age of the universe.

Each breath you take holds about 1022 molecules, and the whole atmosphere about 1044. So if Julius Caesar’s last breath has been mixed evenly through the air over two thousand years, your next breath probably contains about one molecule of it.

1.2Three particles

Every atom is built from just three kinds of particle. Protons and neutrons are packed together in the nucleus at the centre. Electrons spread out around it in a cloud that sets the size of the atom and does all of its chemistry.

The proton’s positive charge and the electron’s negative charge are exactly equal in size. Experiments show the two match to better than one part in 1021, which is why a neutral atom is so thoroughly neutral. But their masses are wildly different.

The number of protons is the atom’s identity card. Six protons make carbon, every time, in a diamond, in your DNA or in a distant star. The number of neutrons can vary without changing the element, producing different isotopes (Chapter 6). The number of electrons can change too: an atom that loses or gains electrons becomes a charged ion, which is how table salt holds together (Chapter 7).

1.3How empty?

The nucleus is tiny even by atomic standards. Measured to where neighbouring atoms bump into each other, a hydrogen atom is about 100,000 times wider than its single proton. Measured only to the electron’s most likely distance, the Bohr radius, it is still about 60,000 times wider. Heavier atoms have bigger nuclei, so the ratio drops, but only to around 20,000 for gold.

Because the nucleus holds 99.95% or more of the mass in roughly a million-billionth of the volume, nuclear matter is extraordinarily dense: about 2 × 1017 kilograms per cubic metre. A sugar cube of it would weigh around 200 million tonnes. Neutron stars are made of exactly this material, packed into a ball about 20 kilometres across.

Yet calling the atom “empty” is misleading. The space is filled by the electron cloud, and electron clouds strongly resist being pushed into each other. When you rest your hand on a table, none of your atoms touch the table’s. The solidity you feel is electrons pushing back, for reasons that come from quantum mechanics (Chapter 3).

1.4Inside the proton

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental. Each is made of three quarks, held together by particles called gluons. Two kinds of quark are enough for ordinary matter: the up quark, with a charge of +⅔, and the down quark, with −⅓. A proton is up, up, down: ⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1. A neutron is up, down, down: ⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.

Here is the strange part. The three quarks in a proton have a combined mass of only about 9 MeV/c². The proton weighs 938 MeV/c². Roughly 99% of its mass is not the mass of its parts at all, but the energy of the quarks’ motion and of the gluon field that confines them, turned into mass as Einstein’s E = mc² allows.

No one has ever seen a quark on its own. Pull two quarks apart and the energy in the gluon field between them grows until it creates new quarks, so you end up with more particles rather than a free quark. Electrons, by contrast, show no sign of having any parts. Experiments have probed them down to 10−18 metres and found nothing inside.
