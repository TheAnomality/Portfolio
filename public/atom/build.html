Build an atom

Add and remove protons, neutrons and electrons. The protons decide the element, the neutrons the isotope, and the electrons the charge. Every nucleus you make is looked up in the IAEA’s database of all known nuclei, so you will find out whether it is stable, how it decays, or whether it has ever been seen at all.

What the three numbers do

Protons set the identity. Change their number and you change the element: six is carbon, seven is nitrogen, no matter what else is present. Neutrons change the mass and, crucially, the stability of the nucleus, but barely affect chemistry. Electrons decide the charge, and whether the atom is neutral, a positive ion that has lost electrons, or a negative ion that has gained them.

Try adding neutrons to carbon one at a time. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable; carbon-14 lasts thousands of years; carbon-16 less than a second; and by carbon-23 nothing has ever been observed. Then take neutrons away: carbon-9 survives for a hundredth of a second, carbon-8 for a few zeptoseconds. Each element has a narrow window of neutron numbers that holds together, and the windows trace out the valley of stability in Chapter 6.

The shell picture used here counts electrons per energy level, but real electrons occupy orbitals, which are clouds rather than rings (Chapter 4).

The ion rules here are simplified. When an atom loses electrons, they come from its outermost shell first; that is why iron loses its 4s electrons before any 3d ones. A free atom can hold at most one extra electron, and only if it has a positive electron affinity. Noble gases, nitrogen and some metals cannot hold even that.
