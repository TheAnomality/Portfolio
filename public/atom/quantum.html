Chapter 3

Quantum leaps

Why an electron bound in an atom can only hold certain energies, and how its jumps between them produce light of exact colours: a fingerprint for every element in the universe.

By 1911 Ernest Rutherford had shown that an atom is a tiny, heavy nucleus surrounded by electrons. It was a triumph, and it was also a disaster. In the physics of the day, an electron circling a nucleus is an accelerating charge, and accelerating charges radiate light. Losing energy with every turn, the electron should spiral inwards and hit the nucleus in about sixteen trillionths of a second. Every atom in the universe should have collapsed almost immediately.

There was a second puzzle. Heat a gas of any element until it glows, pass the light through a prism, and you do not see a smooth rainbow. You see a few sharp, bright lines at precise colours, and the pattern is different for every element. Classical physics could explain neither fact. Both were solved by the same idea: electrons are waves.

3.1Electrons are waves

In 1924 a French doctoral student, Louis de Broglie, proposed that if light can behave like a stream of particles (as Einstein had shown in 1905), then particles might also behave like waves. He gave a formula for the wavelength of anything that moves:

λ=hpde Broglie, 1924

Here h is Planck’s constant and p is momentum. For a thrown baseball the wavelength is absurdly small, around 10−34 metres. For an electron moving at the speed it has inside a hydrogen atom, about 2,200 kilometres per second, the wavelength is 0.33 nanometres: roughly the size of the atom itself. That coincidence is the key to this whole chapter.

In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Bell Labs confirmed the idea by firing electrons at a nickel crystal. The electrons bounced off in a diffraction pattern, exactly as waves would. The most striking demonstration came later, when experimenters sent electrons through a pair of slits one at a time.

A real version of this experiment, with single electrons appearing one by one on a screen, was carried out by Akira Tonomura’s team at Hitachi in 1989 [4].

This is the strangeness at the heart of quantum mechanics, and it is why this site draws electrons as clouds of dots. A single electron is always detected at one place. The wave tells you only how likely each place is.

3.2A wave that must close on itself

Now wrap an electron wave around a nucleus. As it travels round and returns to its starting point, it meets itself. If a whole number of wavelengths fits around the circle, the wave lines up with itself and every lap reinforces the last. If not, it comes back out of step, and over many laps it cancels itself out.

Only certain orbits survive, and each has a definite energy. This is quantisation, and it explains why atoms do not collapse. There is a lowest allowed state, n = 1, and below it there is simply no state to fall into. Niels Bohr had proposed these orbits in 1913, a decade before de Broglie supplied the reason.

The circular standing wave is a stepping stone, not the final theory: real electron waves are three-dimensional (Chapter 4). But for hydrogen it gives exactly the right energies.

3.3Why atoms are the size they are

There is another way to see why the electron cannot fall in, and it also explains the size of atoms. Confining a wave to a smaller space forces it into a shorter wavelength, which means more momentum and more kinetic energy. Werner Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle makes this precise: squeeze an electron into a region of size r and its momentum must spread by at least about ħ/r.

So the electron is caught in a tug-of-war. Moving closer to the nucleus lowers its electrical energy. But confining it more tightly raises its kinetic energy, and that cost grows faster. The atom settles where the total is lowest.

That such a rough argument lands exactly on the right answer is partly luck, but it captures the essential physics. The size of every atom, and therefore of everything built from atoms, is fixed by this balance between quantum confinement and electrical attraction. It is ultimately why matter takes up space at all: electron waves resist being squeezed.

3.4Leaps and light

An electron on a higher level can drop to a lower one. Energy is conserved, so the difference leaves as a single particle of light, a photon, whose energy matches the gap exactly:

Ephoton=Eupper − Elower=h f=hcλ

Big gaps make energetic photons with short wavelengths; small gaps make long-wavelength ones. Because the levels are fixed, so are the colours.

Jumps that end on n = 1 release the most energy and produce ultraviolet light: the Lyman series. Jumps that end on n = 2 form the Balmer series, and its first four lines are visible: red at 656 nm, blue-green at 486 nm, and two violets at 434 and 410 nm. In 1885 a Swiss mathematics teacher, Johann Balmer, found a formula that fitted these four wavelengths without knowing why it worked. Bohr’s model reproduced Balmer’s formula from first principles, and that was when physicists began to take quantum theory seriously.

The red Balmer line, H-alpha, glows wherever starlight excites hydrogen gas. It gives emission nebulae such as the Orion Nebula their pink-red colour in photographs.

3.5Every element has a fingerprint

Other atoms have more electrons and more complicated ladders of levels, but the principle is the same. Each element has its own set of energy levels, so each emits and absorbs its own set of wavelengths. The pattern is as distinctive as a fingerprint, and it identifies an element wherever it glows, from a laboratory flame to a galaxy billions of light years away.

Sodium’s two strong yellow lines, at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, are the colour of old low-pressure street lamps and of table salt sprinkled into a gas flame. Neon’s crowd of red and orange lines gives a neon sign its glow; signs in other colours use other gases or coloured coatings. Mercury’s strongest line is invisible, in the ultraviolet at 254 nm, and it is what powers a fluorescent tube: a coating on the glass absorbs it and re-emits visible light.

3.6Reading the light of stars

In 1814 Joseph von Fraunhofer noticed that the Sun’s spectrum is crossed by hundreds of dark lines, and he charted 574 of them. Nobody knew what they were until 1859, when Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed that a cool gas absorbs exactly the wavelengths it emits when hot. The dark lines in sunlight are atoms in the Sun’s outer layers absorbing light from the hotter surface beneath.

This technique, spectroscopy, is how we know the composition of stars we will never visit. It even revealed an element before anyone found it on Earth. During the solar eclipse of 1868, astronomers saw a bright yellow line at 587.6 nm that matched no known element. Norman Lockyer named the new element helium, from the Greek for Sun. It was finally isolated on Earth in 1895.

3.7What a quantum leap really is

Popular accounts make a “quantum leap” sound instantaneous, as if the electron vanished from one level and reappeared on another. The modern picture is subtler and more beautiful. During a transition the electron is in a superposition, partly in both states at once. The two states have different energies, so their waves oscillate at different frequencies, and where they overlap they beat against each other. The result is a charge cloud that sloshes back and forth at exactly the frequency f = ΔE/h.

A sloshing charge is a tiny antenna, and antennas radiate. The light it gives off has exactly the slosh frequency, and that is why each photon carries the energy of the gap between the two levels. The same picture explains selection rules. Some pairs of states produce a cloud that changes shape without its centre of charge ever moving. Such a cloud cannot radiate like an antenna, so that jump is “forbidden”: it still happens, but by slower routes, often millions of times more slowly.

In 2019 physicists at Yale watched a quantum jump in an artificial atom unfold over a few microseconds, and showed they could catch it mid-flight and reverse it [7].
The chapter in one paragraph

An electron bound to a nucleus is a wave that must fit around it, so only certain energies are allowed. Light is emitted or absorbed only in amounts that match the gaps between those energies, which gives every element a unique spectrum.
