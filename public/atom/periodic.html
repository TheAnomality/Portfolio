Chapter 5

Building the table

Add electrons one at a time, follow three rules, and the periodic table assembles itself. Its strange shape is the shape of quantum mechanics.

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev, a chemistry professor in St Petersburg, arranged the 63 elements then known in order of atomic weight and noticed that their properties repeated at regular intervals. Where the pattern demanded an element nobody had found, he left a gap and predicted what it would be like. He could not have known why the pattern existed. The answer took another sixty years, and it lies in how electrons fill orbitals.

5.1Three rules for filling an atom

Imagine building every element in turn, adding one proton to the nucleus and one electron to the cloud each time. Each new electron settles according to three rules.

  1. Lowest energy first. Each electron takes the lowest-energy orbital still available. This is the aufbau principle, from the German for “building up”.
  2. Two per orbital. No two electrons can share all four quantum numbers (the Pauli exclusion principle from Chapter 4), so an orbital holds two electrons with opposite spins, and no more.
  3. Spread out before pairing. Among orbitals of equal energy, electrons first occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins, and only then double up. Electrons repel each other less when apart. This is Hund’s rule.

5.2Why 4s comes before 3d

The filling order holds a surprise. After argon, the next electron goes into 4s rather than 3d, even though 3 is smaller than 4. In hydrogen, where only n matters, 3d would come first. In larger atoms, inner electrons shield the nucleus, and orbitals that dip close to it feel more of its pull and drop in energy. An s orbital, with its inner lobes (Figure 4.3 shows them), penetrates furthest. The outcome is summed up by a rule of thumb usually credited to Erwin Madelung: orbitals fill in order of n + ℓ, and when two are tied, the one with lower n fills first.

The rule is right for most elements but not all. Of the first 103, twenty have measured ground states that break it, starting with chromium, [Ar] 3d5 4s1 instead of the predicted 3d4 4s2, and copper, [Ar] 3d10 4s1. Near the crossover, the 4s and 3d energies are so close that subtle effects of electron repulsion decide the winner. Step to element 24 in Figure 5.1 to see the first exception flagged.

A further subtlety: once 3d is occupied, it sits below 4s in energy. That is why transition metals lose their 4s electrons first when they form ions. The filling order describes which whole configuration has the lowest total energy, not a fixed ranking of single orbitals [5].

5.3The shape of the table

Now the layout makes sense. The two columns on the left are the s-block: an s subshell holds two electrons. The six columns on the right are the p-block: three p orbitals, six electrons. The ten columns in the middle are the d-block, five d orbitals. The two rows of fourteen set beneath the table are the f-block, seven f orbitals, placed below only so that the table fits on a page. A period ends each time a p subshell fills up, which makes periods of 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements.

Elements in the same column share the same arrangement of outer electrons, and outer electrons are what chemistry is made of. That is why fluorine, chlorine and bromine all grab one extra electron given the chance, why sodium and potassium both give one away, and why helium, neon and argon barely react at all.

5.4Shielding

Every electron feels the charge of the nucleus partly cancelled by the electrons between it and the nucleus. A sodium atom has 11 protons, but its single outer electron sits outside 10 others and feels an effective charge of only about +2. Moving across a period, each element adds one proton but its new electron goes into the same shell, where electrons shield each other poorly, so the effective charge climbs steadily. When a new shell begins, the outer electron suddenly has a whole extra layer of shielding beneath it, and the effective charge falls back.

5.5Periodic trends

Effective charge explains most of what makes the table periodic. A larger effective charge holds the outer electrons more tightly, so they are harder to remove, the atom is smaller, and it pulls harder on electrons it shares with neighbours.

The ionisation energy curve is the most direct evidence for shells that exists. Each peak is a noble gas with a completed shell; each cliff is an alkali metal starting a new one. The little dips inside each period are evidence for sub-shells: boron’s outer electron is easier to remove than beryllium’s because it sits in a 2p orbital, slightly higher in energy than 2s. Oxygen’s is easier to remove than nitrogen’s because oxygen’s fourth 2p electron is forced to share an orbital, and paired electrons repel.

5.6Mendeleev’s gamble

Mendeleev predicted three missing elements in detail, and all three were found within seventeen years: gallium in 1875, scandium in 1879 and germanium in 1886. Here is his 1871 forecast for the element he called eka-silicon, beside the one Clemens Winkler discovered.

PropertyEka-silicon (predicted 1871)Germanium (found 1886)
Atomic weight7272.63
Density5.5 g/cm³5.32 g/cm³

Density of chloride1.9 g/cm³1.88 g/cm³

A pattern that can predict the density of a compound of an undiscovered element is not a coincidence. It took quantum mechanics to show what it was: the periodic table is a map of how electron waves fit around a nucleus.
