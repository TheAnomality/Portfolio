Chapter 4

Orbitals

Solve the Schrödinger equation for a hydrogen atom and these shapes fall out: the real geometry of an electron’s wave, calculated exactly in your browser.

In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger wrote down an equation describing how waves of matter behave, and almost immediately applied it to the simplest atom there is: one proton and one electron. Hydrogen is the only atom whose equation can be solved exactly, with pen and paper. The solutions are called orbitals.

An orbital is not a path. It is a wavefunction, written ψ (psi): a number attached to every point in space. Square it and you get the probability of finding the electron at that point if you look. Until it is measured, the electron does not have a position. ψ is the most complete description of it that physics allows.

4.1Three numbers describe every shape

Each solution carries three whole-number labels. They come from demanding that the wave fit smoothly around the nucleus in all three dimensions, in the same way that a guitar string can only vibrate at notes whose waves fit its length.

In hydrogen, the energy depends only on n: the 3s, 3p and 3d orbitals have exactly the same energy. That tie is broken in every atom with more than one electron.
  • n, the principal quantum number (1, 2, 3, …) sets the energy, E = −13.6 eV / n², and the size. The cloud grows roughly as n².
  • ℓ, the angular momentum number (0 up to n − 1) sets the shape. The values 0, 1, 2, 3 are named s, p, d and f, letters inherited from nineteenth-century descriptions of spectral lines: sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental.
  • m, the magnetic number (from −ℓ to +ℓ) sets the orientation, so there are 2ℓ + 1 of them. The figure uses the real combinations that chemists draw, named after their shapes: px, py, pz, dxy, dz² and so on.
ψnℓm(r, θ, φ)=Rnℓ(r)·Yℓm(θ, φ)

The wavefunction splits into a radial part R, which says how the wave changes with distance from the nucleus, multiplied by an angular part Y, which gives the shape. The figure evaluates exactly these functions, generalised Laguerre polynomials and spherical harmonics, tens of thousands of times a second.

Now count the options for each n: one s orbital, three p, five d, seven f. A shell with principal number n contains exactly n² orbitals. Each orbital can hold at most two electrons (for a reason we will meet below), so the shells hold 2, 8, 18 and 32 electrons. Those four numbers are the widths of the periods of the periodic table, and in Chapter 5 they build it.

4.2Where the electron never is

Most orbitals have nodes: surfaces where ψ passes through zero, so the probability of finding the electron there is exactly zero. They come in two kinds. Radial nodes are spheres centred on the nucleus, and an orbital has n − ℓ − 1 of them. Angular nodes are planes or cones through the nucleus, and there are ℓ of them. Every orbital therefore has n − 1 nodes in total, the same rule that gives a guitar string’s third harmonic two still points.

This raises an old puzzle. In a p orbital, the two lobes are separated by a plane where the electron can never be. How does it get from one lobe to the other? It doesn’t have to. The electron is not a particle shuttling back and forth; it is a standing wave that occupies both lobes at once, in the way a vibrating string moves on both sides of a node without anything ever crossing it.

How far from the nucleus?

|ψ|² is a probability per unit volume. But there is much more volume far from the nucleus than close to it: a thin shell at radius r has an area of 4πr². Multiplying the two gives the radial probability, the chance of finding the electron at a given distance in any direction.

For the ground state, 1s, the radial probability peaks at exactly one Bohr radius, 52.9 picometres. That is the orbit radius Niels Bohr calculated in 1913 for his planet-like model. It survives in quantum mechanics, not as the track of the electron but as its single most likely distance from the nucleus.

4.3Amber and blue: the phase

The two colours mark the sign of ψ. For an isolated atom the sign has no observable effect: only |ψ|² can be measured, and flipping every sign at once changes nothing. Signs start to matter when two waves overlap. Where matching signs meet, the waves add; where opposite signs meet, they cancel. That is essentially the whole story of the chemical bond, which Chapter 7 lets you build.

4.4Spin, the fourth number

Orbitals do not capture everything about an electron. In 1922 Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach fired a beam of silver atoms through a magnetic field that was stronger on one side than the other. Classical physics predicted that tiny atomic magnets, pointing every which way, would smear the beam into a continuous band. Instead it split cleanly in two.

The split was nearly invisible at first. The thin silver deposit only showed up when Stern breathed on the plate: sulfur from his cheap cigars turned the silver into black silver sulfide. [4]

The explanation came a few years later. Electrons carry an intrinsic angular momentum called spin, with exactly two possible values, labelled ms = +½ and −½: “spin up” and “spin down”. In 1925 Wolfgang Pauli stated the rule that structures all of chemistry: no two electrons in an atom can share all four quantum numbers. An orbital is fixed by n, ℓ and m, so it can hold at most two electrons, one of each spin.

4.6Beyond hydrogen

For any atom with more than one electron, the Schrödinger equation cannot be solved exactly, because every electron repels every other one. But an excellent approximation treats each electron as moving in the averaged field of the nucleus and all the other electrons. In that picture every atom has orbitals with the same shapes and the same labels as hydrogen’s, shrunk and shifted in energy. The 2p orbitals of carbon and oxygen look much like hydrogen’s 2p, pulled in more tightly by a larger nuclear charge.

Are orbitals real, or just mathematics? They are mathematics whose predictions are tested constantly, in every spectrum and every chemical bond. In 2013 a team led from Amsterdam went further and used photoionisation microscopy to magnify the electron wave of excited hydrogen atoms onto a detector, revealing the ring-shaped nodal pattern that quantum mechanics predicts [5].
