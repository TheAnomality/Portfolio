Chapter 7

Atoms together

Atoms rarely stay alone. By sharing or handing over a few outer electrons they lock together into molecules and crystals, and that is how 118 kinds of atom become everything else.

A chemical bond is usually drawn as a line, or a stick in a model kit. Like the solar-system atom, the stick is a useful cartoon and a poor description. A real bond is a region where electron waves from two atoms overlap and reinforce, pulling both nuclei towards the electron density that builds up between them.

7.1The simplest bond

Bring two hydrogen atoms together. Far apart, each has its own 1s electron cloud. As they approach, the clouds overlap, and the two electron waves can combine in two ways (Chapter 4 explained the role of their signs). In phase, the waves add up between the nuclei, piling up electron density there. Both nuclei are attracted to that negative charge, and the atoms hold together. Out of phase, the waves cancel between the nuclei, leaving a node. The nuclei are then left exposed to each other’s repulsion.

The energy curve tells the whole story. Pull the atoms apart and the energy rises towards zero, the energy of two separate atoms. Push them too close and the nuclei repel. In between there is a valley. At the bottom, 74 picometres apart, the molecule is 4.75 eV lower in energy than two separate atoms. That valley is the bond, and its depth is why hydrogen gas is made of H2 molecules rather than loose atoms.

This explanation was the first triumph of quantum chemistry: Walter Heitler and Fritz London worked it out in 1927, a year after Schrödinger’s equation appeared.

The same picture explains why helium does not form molecules. Each helium atom brings two electrons, so a helium pair would have to put two electrons in the in-phase state and two in the out-of-phase state. The gain and the loss cancel, and the atoms drift apart. (A fragile helium pair, bound by about a ten-millionth of an electronvolt, has been detected near absolute zero, but it is the exception that proves the rule.)

7.2Sharing unequally

When the two atoms are different, the shared electrons are rarely shared evenly. Some atoms pull harder: they have a larger effective nuclear charge acting on their outer electrons (Chapter 5). Linus Pauling turned this into a number in 1932, electronegativity, and the difference between two atoms’ values tells you which way, and how far, the electrons lean.

In a water molecule, oxygen (3.44) pulls the shared electrons away from the two hydrogens (2.20), leaving the oxygen end slightly negative and the hydrogen ends slightly positive. Because the molecule is bent, at 104.5°, these partial charges do not cancel. Water molecules therefore stick to one another by their opposite ends, and that weak attraction is why water is a liquid at room temperature when much heavier molecules, such as carbon dioxide, are gases.

7.3Handing electrons over

When the electronegativity difference is large enough, one atom effectively takes the electron outright. Sodium’s single outer electron is loosely held; chlorine is one electron short of a full shell. The result is a positive sodium ion and a negative chloride ion, held together by plain electrical attraction. But the bookkeeping holds a surprise.

Moving an electron from a sodium atom to a chlorine atom costs energy on its own: sodium gives it up for 5.14 eV, but chlorine only returns 3.62 eV. A lone pair of ions is higher in energy than the two atoms. What pays the bill is the crystal. Each ion settles among neighbours of opposite charge, and the electrical attraction of the whole lattice releases 8.2 eV per pair, far more than the cost of the transfer. Ionic bonding is a collective effect.

7.4Metals and their sea of electrons

In a metal, each atom releases one or more outer electrons into a shared pool that spreads through the entire piece of metal. The positive ions sit in a lattice, bathed in this mobile electron sea. That one arrangement explains the familiar properties of metals. The free electrons carry electric current and heat; they reflect light, which makes metals shiny; and because the bonding is not tied to particular pairs of atoms, layers of ions can slide past each other without breaking apart, which is why metals bend rather than shatter.

7.5The shapes of molecules

Bonds also have directions. The pairs of electrons around an atom, whether shared in bonds or not, repel one another and spread out as far as they can. Four pairs around a carbon atom point to the corners of a tetrahedron, so methane’s bonds meet at 109.5°. In ammonia one of the four pairs is not a bond, and it squeezes the angle between the others to 107°; in water, two non-bonding pairs squeeze it to 104.5°. Carbon dioxide, with no spare pairs on its carbon, is a straight line. Shape matters enormously: it decides how molecules fit together, which is the basis of everything from the stickiness of water to the way enzymes recognise their targets.

7.6From atoms to you

A human body contains roughly 7 × 1027 atoms. Counted one by one, about 62% are hydrogen, 24% oxygen and 12% carbon, with nitrogen making up most of the rest. Nearly all the hydrogen was made in the first few minutes after the Big Bang. The carbon, nitrogen and oxygen were forged later inside stars, by the fusion reactions of Chapter 6, and scattered into space when those stars died.

Every one of those atoms is the kind of object this guide has described: a nucleus tens of thousands of times smaller than itself, wrapped in electron waves whose shapes are fixed by quantum mechanics, bound to its neighbours by the overlap of those waves. That such a small set of rules can produce a person reading about them is, perhaps, the most remarkable fact about atoms of all.
