Chapter 2

How we found out

For two thousand years atoms were an idea. Then, in about thirty years, they were weighed, measured, split open and described by a new kind of physics. A story of models and the experiments that broke them.

As late as 1900, some respected scientists argued that atoms might be nothing more than a convenient fiction, a bookkeeping device for chemists. Nobody had seen one, and nobody knew what one looked like inside. What followed is one of the great detective stories in science. Each picture of the atom explained the evidence of its day, and each was overturned by an experiment it could not explain.

2.1An old idea

In the fifth century BCE the Greek philosophers Leucippus and Democritus argued that if you kept cutting matter into smaller pieces you would eventually reach pieces that could not be cut: atomos, “uncuttable”. It was a brilliant guess, but only a guess. There were no experiments, and for most of the next two thousand years the idea was a minority opinion.

2.2Atoms with weights

The idea became science in 1808, when the English chemist John Dalton noticed that elements always combine in simple, fixed ratios by mass. Water always contains eight grams of oxygen for every gram of hydrogen. Carbon forms two gases with oxygen, and the masses of oxygen per gram of carbon in them are in a ratio of exactly two to one. Dalton’s explanation was that each element consists of identical atoms with a characteristic weight, and that compounds are atoms joined in whole-number combinations.

Doubts lingered for another century. They were finally settled by a puzzle under the microscope: pollen grains suspended in water jiggle about endlessly. In 1905 Albert Einstein showed that this Brownian motion is the grains being jostled by invisible molecules, and predicted how far they should wander. In 1908 Jean Perrin measured the jiggling, worked backwards to the size of molecules, and obtained Avogadro’s number. Even the most determined sceptics accepted that atoms were real.

2.3Atoms have parts

Meanwhile, in 1897, J. J. Thomson at Cambridge had been studying cathode rays, the glowing beams inside evacuated glass tubes. By bending them with electric and magnetic fields he showed they were streams of negatively charged particles more than a thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom, and that every metal he tried produced the same particles. He had discovered the electron, and with it the fact that atoms are not uncuttable after all.

Atoms are electrically neutral, so the electrons must be balanced by positive charge somewhere. Thomson proposed in 1904 that the positive charge was spread throughout the atom like a pudding, with the electrons embedded in it like plums. It was a reasonable guess. It was also wrong.

2.4The gold foil

Ernest Rutherford, working in Manchester, had a new kind of probe: alpha particles, fast and heavy positively charged particles shot out by radioactive elements. In 1909 he asked his colleague Hans Geiger and an undergraduate, Ernest Marsden, to fire them at gold foil less than a thousandth of a millimetre thick and record where they went, by watching for tiny flashes of light on a screen in a darkened room.

Almost all the alpha particles went straight through, as the pudding model predicted. But Geiger and Marsden noticed that about one in every eight thousand bounced back towards the source. Rutherford later recalled:

It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.Ernest Rutherford, recalling the experiment in a lecture in 1936

Spread-out charge can only nudge a fast alpha particle by a tiny fraction of a degree. To turn one right round, the atom’s positive charge, and almost all its mass, must be packed into a minute central region where the electric field is enormous. Rutherford worked out how often such a nucleus would deflect alpha particles through each angle, and in 1913 Geiger and Marsden confirmed his formula in detail. The atom was mostly empty, with a tiny heavy heart.

Try a low energy with the nuclear model. Slower alpha particles are turned back from farther away, so more of them scatter widely. Rutherford’s formula predicts exactly how strongly, and experiments matched it.

The same year, a young physicist in Rutherford’s group, Henry Moseley, measured the X-rays given off by dozens of elements and found that their frequencies climbed in perfect steps from one element to the next. The step was one unit of nuclear charge. Each element’s place in the periodic table is fixed not by its weight but by the number of protons in its nucleus, its atomic number. Moseley was killed at Gallipoli in 1915, aged 27.

2.5The quantum atom

Rutherford’s atom had a fatal flaw. Electrons circling a nucleus should radiate their energy away and spiral inwards almost instantly. In 1913 Niels Bohr, who had worked with Rutherford, proposed a radical fix: electrons can only move on certain orbits, and lose energy only by jumping between them. It was an unexplained rule, but it reproduced hydrogen’s spectrum perfectly. Within thirteen years, Louis de Broglie, Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger had replaced Bohr’s orbits with quantum mechanics, in which electrons are waves (Chapter 3 and Chapter 4).

One piece remained. Nuclei weighed roughly twice as much as their protons could account for. In 1932 James Chadwick, another of Rutherford’s colleagues, identified the missing mass: a neutral particle with almost the same mass as the proton, the neutron (Chapter 6).

2.6Seeing atoms

All of this was worked out without anyone seeing an atom. The first images of individual atoms came in 1955, when Erwin Müller and Kanwar Bahadur used a field ion microscope to photograph the atoms at the tip of a tungsten needle. In 1981 Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM in Zurich built the scanning tunnelling microscope, which feels its way across a surface with a needle one atom sharp. It earned them the 1986 Nobel Prize, and it can also move atoms: in 1989 Don Eigler and Erhard Schweizer spelled “IBM” with 35 xenon atoms.

Today, electron microscopes routinely resolve individual atoms, and in 2021 a technique called electron ptychography reached a resolution of about 20 picometres, limited mainly by the atoms’ own thermal jiggling. The fiction of 1900 is now something we can photograph, count and rearrange one at a time.
