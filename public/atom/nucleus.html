Chapter 6

The nucleus

Tens of thousands of times narrower than the atom, yet holding nearly all of its mass. How the nucleus holds together, why most combinations of protons and neutrons fall apart, and where the energy of the Sun comes from.

Everything chemists care about happens in the electron cloud. The nucleus just sits at the centre, supplying positive charge and almost all of the mass. But inside that speck is a second world with its own forces, its own shell structure, and energies millions of times larger than any chemical reaction.

6.1Protons, neutrons and isotopes

A nucleus contains protons and neutrons, together called nucleons. The number of protons, Z, fixes the element: every atom with six protons is carbon. The number of neutrons, N, can vary, and atoms of the same element with different neutron counts are called isotopes. They are written with the total number of nucleons, the mass number A = Z + N: carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons, carbon-13 has seven neutrons, and carbon-14 has eight.

Because isotopes have the same number of electrons, they behave almost identically in chemistry. Their nuclei can be completely different. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable forever; carbon-14 decays, with half of any sample gone after 5,700 years.

Hydrogen’s isotopes are the only ones with their own names: protium (no neutrons), deuterium (one) and tritium (two, radioactive, half-life 12.3 years).

6.2What holds it together?

A nucleus should not exist. Its protons are all positively charged and packed about a femtometre apart, and at that distance the electrical repulsion between two of them is about 230 newtons: roughly the weight of a 23-kilogram suitcase, acting on particles that weigh 10−27 kg. Something much stronger must be holding them in place.

That something is the strong nuclear force, a leftover of the force that binds quarks inside each proton and neutron. It attracts protons and neutrons alike, and at a separation of about one femtometre it overwhelms the electrical repulsion. But it has a very short reach. A couple of femtometres further out it has all but disappeared, while the electrical repulsion, which fades only slowly, carries on.

This mismatch shapes the whole nucleus. Each nucleon is held only by its immediate neighbours, but every proton repels every other proton in the nucleus. In a small nucleus that repulsion is easily outweighed. In a big one, the repulsion adds up and adds up, which is why no stable nucleus has more than 82 protons.

6.3Neutrons are the glue

Neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding any electrical repulsion. Light nuclei are most stable with roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons: helium-4, carbon-12 and oxygen-16 all have N = Z. Heavier nuclei need an increasing surplus of neutrons to hold their protons together. Lead-208, the heaviest stable nucleus, has 82 protons and 126 neutrons.

But neutrons cannot be added without limit either. A nucleus with too many neutrons turns one of them into a proton (beta-minus decay); one with too few turns a proton into a neutron. Only a narrow band of combinations survives.

6.4The map of all nuclei

Plot every known nucleus by its numbers of protons and neutrons and you get the chart of nuclides, the nuclear physicist’s equivalent of the periodic table. It holds nearly 3,400 nuclei. Only 244 have never been seen to decay. A few dozen more decay so slowly that they have survived since before the Earth formed, which is why uranium and thorium are still found in the ground.

Look closely and you will see faint bands at 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 and 126. Nuclei with these “magic” numbers of protons or neutrons are unusually stable. Tin, with 50 protons, has ten stable isotopes, more than any other element. The explanation, worked out independently by Maria Goeppert Mayer and by Hans Jensen’s group in 1949, is that nucleons also fill shells, just as electrons do in atoms. It earned them the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Bismuth-209 was long counted as stable. In 2003 it was caught decaying, with a half-life of about 2 × 1019 years: more than a billion times the age of the universe.

6.5Missing mass: E = mc²

Weigh a helium-4 atom and you find something strange: it is lighter than the two hydrogen atoms and two neutrons it is made from, by about 0.75%. The missing mass has not vanished. It left as energy when the nucleus formed, according to Einstein’s E = mc², and it is exactly the energy you would need to supply to pull the nucleus apart again. This is the binding energy.

This curve is one of the most important graphs in physics. It rises steeply through the light elements, peaks around iron and nickel, and slowly falls for the heaviest nuclei. Anything that moves nuclei uphill releases energy. Fusing light nuclei moves them up the left side: that is how stars shine. Splitting heavy nuclei moves them up the right side: that is how nuclear reactors and atomic bombs work. The single most tightly bound nucleus is nickel-62, just ahead of iron-58 and iron-56.

The amounts are enormous. Fusing four hydrogen nuclei into helium, as the Sun does, converts 0.7% of their mass into energy: 26.7 MeV for each helium nucleus made. Burning a carbon atom releases about 4 eV. To power its output, the Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of matter into energy every second.

6.6Radioactive decay

Unstable nuclei change into more stable ones by emitting particles. The main routes are:

  • Alpha decay: the nucleus spits out a helium-4 nucleus (two protons, two neutrons). Common among heavy nuclei, which are trying to shed protons.
  • Beta-minus decay: a neutron becomes a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino. Nuclei with too many neutrons do this.
  • Beta-plus decay and electron capture: a proton becomes a neutron, either emitting a positron or absorbing one of the atom’s own inner electrons. Nuclei with too few neutrons do this.
  • Gamma emission: a nucleus left in an excited state drops to a lower one and emits a high-energy photon, exactly like an atom emitting light (Chapter 3), only a million times more energetic.
  • Spontaneous fission: a very heavy nucleus simply splits in two.

There is no way to predict when a particular nucleus will decay. What is fixed is the probability: in any one half-life, each nucleus has an even chance of decaying. With enough nuclei, that randomness averages into a smooth, perfectly predictable curve.

This is what makes radioactive dating possible. Living things take in carbon-14 from the air; when they die, the intake stops and the carbon-14 starts to dwindle. A sample with a quarter of its original carbon-14 has been dead for two half-lives, about 11,400 years. The same arithmetic with uranium-238 and its decay product lead-206 dates the oldest rocks on Earth at around 4 billion years.

6.7Decay chains

Often the product of a decay is itself unstable, and decays in turn. Uranium-238 passes through fourteen steps, eight alpha decays and six beta decays, before ending as stable lead-206. Along the way it makes radium, which Marie and Pierre Curie discovered in 1898, and radon, the gas that seeps into basements from granite.

6.8Splitting and joining

When a uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a slow neutron it can split into two mid-sized nuclei plus two or three new neutrons, releasing about 200 MeV. If enough of the new neutrons go on to split further nuclei, the reaction sustains itself. Controlled, that is a nuclear power station. A kilogram of uranium-235, fully fissioned, releases about as much energy as burning 3,000 tonnes of coal.

Fusion goes the other way. Joining a deuterium nucleus to a tritium nucleus makes helium-4 and a neutron, releasing 17.6 MeV. It is the reaction that fusion reactors aim to harness: the fuel is abundant, and the reaction makes no long-lived radioactive waste of its own, although its neutrons do make the reactor’s walls radioactive. The obstacle is the electrical repulsion from Figure 6.1: the nuclei must be slammed together hard enough to reach the range of the strong force, which takes temperatures above 100 million kelvin.
